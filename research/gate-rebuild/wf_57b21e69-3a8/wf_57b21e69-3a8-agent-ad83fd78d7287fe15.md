**The factual half checks out; the prescriptive half is refuted by the same paper.**

The quote is real and correctly scoped to **contemporaneous** impact. But the claim then leaps: *"so multi-level own-book OFI should be built before any cross-sectional order-flow machinery."* Cont/Cucuringu/Zhang's abstract contains a second finding the claim omits: **"lagged cross-asset OFIs do improve the forecasting of future returns"**, with the effect concentrated at short horizons and decaying rapidly. Contemporaneous explanation and forward prediction are different objects — a null result in one does not transfer to the other. A trade-entry edge model is *purely* a forecasting problem, so the claim cites the one finding that doesn't apply to the use case and drops the one that does.

Two further scope problems:
- **Asset class mismatch.** The study is Nasdaq equities. The requesting context is crypto perps across 200 coins — a fragmented, multi-venue market. The Bitcoin-specific literature ([Fragmentation, Price Formation and Cross-Impact in Bitcoin Markets](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1350486X.2022.2080083)) finds the opposite of the claim's prescription: flow arrives on one venue and "trickles down" to others, so the cross-section of trade flows carries explanatory power over *future* returns. Crypto's fragmentation is precisely the condition that makes cross-asset/cross-venue flow informative — the mechanism is absent in the consolidated-tape equity setting the claim generalizes from.
- **The "subsumed" framing is a misread.** The paper shows cross-impact is redundant *for decomposing simultaneous price moves*; it never shows multi-level own-book OFI contains the cross-asset information. Its own lagged results show it does not.

The build-ordering advice may still be defensible on other grounds (multi-level OFI is cheaper and the paper does show it beats best-level OFI), but it is not supported by the cited quote, which is the claim as stated.
