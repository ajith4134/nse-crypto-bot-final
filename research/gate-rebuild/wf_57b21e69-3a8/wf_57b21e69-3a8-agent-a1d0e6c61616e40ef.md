**Key finding:** The paper's abstract/intro overstate what its own tables show, and the claim inherits that overstatement.

From the extracted PDF (Nasdaq ITCH/LOBSTER, 100 S&P500 large-caps, 2017-01-01 to 2019-12-31, 1-min buckets):

- **Table 1** (multi-level OFI, in-sample R²): PI[5]=87.66, PI[6]=88.30, PI[7]=88.74, PI[8]=89.04, PI[9]=89.24, PI[10]=89.38
- **Table 3** (integrated/PCA OFI): in-sample R² = **87.14**, OOS R² = 83.83

So PII is *below* PI[5..10] in-sample. The paper says so explicitly on p.12: *"even though the in-sample R2 of PII is lower than PI[m] (when m≥ 5), its performance in out-of-sample tests is better than PI[m](∀1≤m≤ 10)."* This directly contradicts the intro's *"superior to both best-level OFIs and multi-level OFIs, in both in-sample and out-of-sample tests"* (p.2). PII is a rank-1 restriction of the nested PI[10], so it **cannot** beat it in-sample — the abstract's wording is mathematically impossible against the multi-level comparator.

The claim's gloss ("the dimensionality reduction is not an in-sample artifact") **inverts the mechanism**. PCA's benefit is *purely* out-of-sample regularization, bought by paying an in-sample cost. The paper's own words: *"the ability of PCA to extract information from data and reduce the chance of overfitting."*

**Most damaging for the research question's actual purpose:** the 87%/83.83% figures are **contemporaneous** price impact — same-bucket returns, a nowcast, not tradeable. The paper's own forecasting section (§5.3) shows integrated OFI predicting *future* 1-min returns:

```
        F-PI (%)   F-CI (%)
ofi^I  In-Sample     0.26      12.19
       Out-Of-Sample -17.62    -11.81
```

F-PII OOS R² = **−17.62%** (negative), vs −19.07% for best-level. As a trade-entry predictor, integrated OFI has negative OOS R² *in the very paper cited*. Anyone importing "83.83% OOS R²" into an entry-edge model would be importing a nowcast identity.

Scope caveat: US equities only, zero crypto perp evidence.
